01

Why jets, and why the trigger

When protons collide at the LHC, quarks and gluons cannot be seen on their own. They break up into a narrow spray of particles called a jet, which travels roughly in the direction of the original quark or gluon. The direction and momentum of a jet tell physicists what happened in the collision, so jets are useful even at the very first stage of selecting events.

A jet is harder to rebuild than a single particle. It contains many particles, and each one leaves its own shower in the calorimeter, so the energy ends up spread over many cells and several layers of the detector.

Field note · pile-up

Each bunch crossing holds more than one proton collision. The interesting one is called the hard scatter, and the extra ones are called pile-up. A calorimeter cell cannot tell which collision its energy came from, so the jets we care about sit on top of a diffuse background.

Field note · the trigger

Bunches cross about 40 million times per second, and the detector cannot record all of them. The trigger decides in real time which events to keep. At the High-Luminosity LHC, up to about 200 collisions will happen in each crossing.

In the new design, a hardware Level-0 trigger reduces the 40 MHz input to up to 1 MHz, and a software Event Filter brings it down to about 10 kHz for storage.

The Global Trigger is part of Level-0 and sees the calorimeter at full detail. Its FPGAs and AI Engines may have room to do some reconstruction before the data leaves the board. That opportunity is what this project explored.

02

Fitting an event into the luggage limit

The goal was to take the calorimeter image of an event and turn it, inside the trigger, into a short list of jets. Each jet is described by its transverse momentum, its position in the detector and a score that says how likely it is to be real.

The events are simulations of Higgs boson pairs, with both Higgs bosons decaying into b quarks and about 200 pile-up collisions in each crossing. The 24 sampling regions of the ATLAS calorimeter are grouped by depth into six channels, and each channel becomes a 50 by 64 grid. That gives 19 200 values per event.

The bandwidth limit

The Global Trigger uses 49 processing boards, so each board handles about 816 000 events per second. With about 20 Gb/s per board, there is room for roughly 24 500 bits per event, or about 766 values at 32-bit precision. Sending the full calorimeter image would need about 502 Gb/s per board, around 25 times the budget.

03

What the calorimeter shows on its own

Before training any network, I measured how far a very simple method could go. A jet usually leaves a local peak of energy, so the six channels were summed, averaged over 2 by 2 blocks, and every cell brighter than its eight neighbours was kept as a candidate. Nothing in this method is learned.

Each peak gets a visibility score that compares its energy with the median and the largest peak of the event. Raising the visibility threshold removes false peaks, but it also starts to lose real jets.

Field glossary
Efficiency
The share of real jets that were found.
Purity
The share of kept candidates that are real jets.
AUC
How well the score separates real candidates from false ones. 1 is perfect and 0.5 is a coin toss.
Matching
A candidate counts as a real jet only within ΔR < 0.4 of one, with a one-to-one assignment from the Hungarian algorithm.

04

A first model with 708 parameters

The model always returns 20 candidate slots. Each slot has a transverse momentum, a position and a score between 0 and 1, and only the slots above a threshold are kept. A fixed output can then describe events with different numbers of jets, which in this sample was usually four to six and at most eleven.

Two small convolutions read the calorimeter image and produce three maps over the detector. One gives the score, one the energy and one a small correction to the position inside each cell. The 20 strongest local peaks of the score map become the candidates. The whole model holds 708 parameters, about 2.8 kB.

Positions came out very precise, with median errors of 0.029 in η and 0.022 in φ, smaller than one cell of the grid. The transverse momentum was less precise, with a median error of 11.4 GeV.

Where the jets are lost

About 95% of the real jets already had a candidate nearby. Most of the loss came afterwards, when the score had to separate weak jets from pile-up. Above 70 GeV the efficiency passed 97%, while many low-energy jets were rejected by the score cut.

The clue that led to attention

Of the 8 209 real jets that had a candidate but failed the score cut, 1 971 were still among the top candidates of their own event. Their score was low in absolute terms but high compared with the rest of the event. The baseline could not use this, because it scores each candidate on its own.

24% of the rejected real jets
05

Letting candidates compare with each other

The next model keeps the same idea and adds a comparison step. The 32 strongest peaks become candidates, each described by 22 values. One self-attention block with four heads lets every candidate look at the others before its score is corrected. Attention only changes the score, while energy and position still come from the convolutional stage.

Efficiency rose from 77.3% to 80.5% and the AUC from 0.934 to 0.950, with the largest gains at low and intermediate energies. The number of real jets lost while ranked near the top of their event fell from 1 971 to 1 577.

This model also has a wider convolutional stage, so the gain cannot be credited to attention alone. The lighter versions below helped separate the two.

V1 went back to the small four-channel convolutions but kept the full attention block, and with 9 639 parameters it reached 80.3%. V2 also shrank the attention block and reached 79.5% with 1 155 parameters. Comparing candidates mattered more for telling jets apart than a wide convolutional stage did.

A side effect worth knowing

With attention, events with three to five jets kept their weakest real jet more often, but events with seven jets lost about 17 points. The model pulled every event towards the usual number of jets in the sample, and the correlation between predicted and true jet counts dropped from 0.543 to 0.512.

06

The same model without pile-up

To find out whether the limit came from the model or from the data, I retrained the same attention model on events with no pile-up. This is only a control test, since the HL-LHC will run at high pile-up.

The jump was much larger than anything the changes to the architecture gave. Most of the ambiguity comes from the calorimeter information itself under high pile-up, and not from the size of the network. Even without pile-up, the lowest energy bin reached only 62.2% efficiency with 95.4% acceptance, so part of the difficulty remains.

The final list of 20 jets with four values each holds 80 values per event. That is about 2.09 Gb/s per board, well below the 20 Gb/s budget.

Bandwidth answers only half of the question. The models were built in TensorFlow, and some of their parts do not convert directly with the standard hls4ml flow. Resource use and latency can only be measured after rewriting those parts and running synthesis on the target FPGA.

CNES · Toulouse · February–August 2025

CNES: six months in Toulouse

A student satellite, a difficult choice and a group of friends in Toulouse, during my last semester in France.

Before Toulouse, there were around 200 applications and a very practical worry: I needed to save money to finish university in Brazil. When two offers finally arrived, the decision wasn’t obvious. I chose CNES, and those six months gave me a satellite to work on, friends from different countries and a city I loved living in.

My flight software work at CNES →

01

Before the offer, the applications

I was in my final year in France and looking for a six-month, full-time internship. Afterwards, I would return to Brazil to finish my aerospace engineering degree. I wasn’t a Fundação Estudar scholar yet, and I needed to put money aside to support myself when I got back. The pay wasn’t a small detail in my search.

I also really wanted to work with satellites or launch vehicles. I had already done research internships and wanted to get closer to that work. I sent around 200 applications. Some led to interviews, sometimes even the final stage, but there were many rejections. At career fairs, I also heard that certain roles weren’t open to me because I didn’t have French or European citizenship, especially when the work involved defence. I had to learn that a rejection didn’t always tell me something about my technical ability.

02

Two offers, one last semester

Eventually, I had two offers: CERN and CNES. The CERN role paid more and involved physics and electronics, with a team I really liked. I would have been the only intern on that project, working with senior engineers. It was an exciting possibility.

At CNES, the pay was lower, but the project was a satellite developed by students. I had loved meeting the team, and I knew there would be other interns sharing that experience. Both choices meant moving to a new city. I kept thinking about the work I wanted to do, but also about how I wanted to spend those last months in France.

I chose CNES. The recruitment process itself had been fairly straightforward; the difficult part was everything I was weighing up around it. This was a decision in 2025. My CERN openlab summer, which I tell elsewhere in this diary, would come in 2026.

03

AEROSAT, beyond the application

AEROSAT is a student nanosatellite project at CNES, within Nanolab Academy. That was one of the things that drew me in: we were students, with support from experienced people, developing a real satellite intended for launch. Knowing that gave the work a very concrete purpose.

I worked on flight software: how the system recovered after a reset, read its memory and organised its tasks. One part involved restoring saved state from FRAM, a memory that retains data without power. I also worked on moving non-critical reads into the background so they wouldn’t hold up startup. These are the technical details behind the experience, and I’ve kept them together on the project page.

04

Having room to ask

My supervisors were always there to help, and we also had a lot of freedom. That combination made a difference. I could take responsibility for my work without feeling that I had to figure everything out alone.

We had weekly meetings, and they encouraged us to ask about the space agency itself, to make the most of being there. I liked that invitation. I was learning to do my work, but I could also ask about the place around me and the people who made it run.

05

Space outside work, too

Toulouse was a wonderful place to live while working in space. My curiosity didn’t stop when I left work. I visited museums and exhibitions, saw rockets and spacecraft displays, and also explored aviation: an A380, Concorde, a cockpit. These photos are part of how I remember that period.

I liked being able to see things up close that I had spent so much time studying. And I liked that discovering the city could include all of this, as well as a concert or an afternoon by the river.

06

The people in these photos

I shared an apartment, made French friends and friends from other countries, and spoke French throughout the day. With so many interns, we became close and spent a lot of time together outside work too. There were meals, picnics, walks and plans around the city. Looking at these photos, I remember how much we managed to fit into those months.

It was intense, and we learned a lot. But we also had so much fun. When I was deciding between the two offers, I had hoped to find that kind of company. Getting to live it was one of the best parts of the choice.

07

What I brought back

Having CNES on my CV opened doors. I felt that in the opportunities that came afterwards. But it was also where I built experience working on a satellite with people who knew so much and were willing to teach us. I came back to Brazil with more than a line on my CV to explain.

The next part of this story was my return to Brazil and Visiona. After speaking French every day and learning so much engineering in that language, even explaining my work in Portuguese took some getting used to. That story has its own page here.

What stays with me

When I think about CNES, I remember the work, but I also remember who was there with me. Money mattered a lot in that decision, and I don’t want to pretend otherwise. What made the choice worthwhile for me was being able to learn about satellites in a team where I felt comfortable asking questions, while making the most of my last months in France. If you’re choosing an internship, ask about the everyday work too: who will help you, who you’ll work alongside and how much room you’ll have to learn. Those answers mattered to me as much as the project description.

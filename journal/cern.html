My summer at CERN

Three applications, an email I almost missed, and nine weeks of work, travel and new friends.

Emilly Rodrigues / From my personal photo album

I had known about CERN for years. Before the photos at ATLAS, the hikes and the friends, there were two unsuccessful applications. On my third attempt, I was rejected again. So my summer at CERN actually starts with an email I nearly dismissed.

01

The third application

I had applied twice before and been rejected both times. For the third attempt, I spent months preparing. I studied what the programme was looking for and messaged people who had already been through the process.

I tried to put all that motivation into my application, to show how much I wanted to be there. On 2 April, the answer came: another rejection. Almost a month passed, and I had more or less put it out of my mind.

02

The email I nearly missed

On 30 April 2026, I woke up to an email from CERN openlab. I had just got up and, because I had already been rejected, I thought it was another automatic rejection message. It was only when I started reading about the project that I realised what had happened: I had been accepted.

The email explained that there had been changes in one of the projects. I had been selected from nearly 6,000 valid applications for nine weeks at CERN, working on “Trigger Level Analysis data preparation through co-processors”.

It was incredible. And then came the rush to buy tickets and organise the trip. Once there, I shared a room with a girl from Mexico, which was really lovely. The programme team arranged everything for us.

03

What I worked on

My project was called “Trigger Level Analysis Data Preparation through Co-processors”. I worked on it with supervisors Ioannis Xiotidis and Noah Clarke Hall, within the Next-Generation Triggers activities for ATLAS.

The question was quite concrete: could we turn a large amount of calorimeter data into a small list of jets, keeping useful physics information while reducing the data we needed to transmit? Jets are groups of particles reconstructed from their detector signals. I investigated small machine-learning models with the constraints of a future FPGA implementation in mind.

I started with a convolutional baseline of 708 trainable parameters, then studied attention between candidates and the effect of overlapping collisions, known as pile-up. The baseline reached 77.3% efficiency at 78.0% purity; adding candidate-level attention brought efficiency to about 80.5%. The compact output was estimated at about 2.1 Gb/s per board, below the assumed 20 Gb/s limit. Hardware synthesis and latency measurements were still next steps, not completed results.

04

The visits and the classes

I visited ATLAS, the experiment my work was connected to. I also visited ALICE and the Antimatter Factory. These photos show very different corners of CERN, each with its own equipment and experiments.

And yes, one of those classroom photos is from a lecture with the creator of C++. That definitely belongs in this diary.

05

I also packed my hiking shoes

There were a lot of mountains that summer. I went hiking, travelled with friends and took plenty of photos along the way. The lake, the paths and the views in this album are part of that side of the experience.

I went to Spain to watch the World Cup final. I also have memories from Barcelona, a visit to Einstein’s house in Bern, Nobel medals on display and an evening at an open-air cinema. There was plenty to do outside the lab.

06

And then there were the people

I met incredible friends during the programme. There are photos at the table, on campus, out together and up in the mountains. I wanted those moments here too, alongside the report and the work. They are part of how I remember my time at CERN.

When I tell someone about CERN, it is easy to start with the experiments and the project. But I also want to remember who was around me during those weeks. The friends, the shared room and the trips belong in this story just as much as the work I went there to do.

What stays with me

I still think about that email I almost dismissed. Preparing carefully hadn’t stopped the rejection, and being accepted later depended on a change I couldn’t control. But talking to former participants and taking the application seriously were things I could do. If you are considering applying, that is where I would start: understand the project, ask questions and give yourself the chance to be considered. I’m glad I didn’t decide on their behalf that I didn’t belong there.
